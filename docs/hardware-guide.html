What to buy for live campy + SLEAP + triangulation

Three machines, with four GPUs between them, were measured running campy recordings and sleap-nn top-down inference (centroid + centered-instance) at the same time, at 3 and 8 cameras. The deciding factors were the inference GPU, CPU speed, and the operating system. Memory channels didn't matter. The full live pipeline, with trained models and 3D tracking of 5 mice, was then run on the EVO-X2 and the Pro Max Slim.

Recommendations

  1. Buy a discrete NVIDIA GPU for inference, and plug no monitor into it.

    The RTX 6000 Ada met 60 fps at p99 in every one of 32 model-size combinations at 3 cameras and in 28 of 32 at 8 cameras. The RTX 2000 Ada met it in at most 6 of 32 when idle, and while recording in none unpinned and 8 of 32 with its CPU threads pinned (below). With its clock held up, the live pipeline's trained pair now runs in 13 ms on it, 60 fps included (below).

    The RTX 4000 Ada in the same workstation showed a steady 18 ms p99 floor in every configuration. It is the card the monitor is plugged into, and Windows' desktop work on that GPU is the likely cause, so its own capability is still untested.

  2. Pick a CPU with fast cores, and at least 8 of them for 3 cameras plus inference.

    On Windows, each set launches about 450 small GPU kernels and waits for the GPU about 20 times, so on a fast GPU latency follows the CPU. Without CUDA graphs, every model size on the RTX 6000 Ada landed between 7 and 9 ms: a CPU-side floor, not GPU compute.

    Squeezing 3 camera encoders and inference onto 6 cores raised the RTX 6000 Ada's p99 from 7.7 to 11.5 ms. Watching the tracking live (campy-live-view) adds a separate, low-priority program that needs 7–9 ms of one core per picture it draws: leave a core for it.

  3. On an Intel hybrid CPU, pin inference to the performance cores and raise its priority.

    While recording, Windows ran the Pro Max Slim's inference partly on its efficiency cores. Pinning inference to the 8 performance cores, moving campy and its encoders to the 16 efficiency cores and running inference at High priority took the four fastest model pairs from 17.6 ms median and 23.5 ms p99 to 11.8 and 12.3 ms. Across all 32 model sizes, 8 then fit 60 fps while recording 3 cameras at 80 fps (none did unpinned), all 32 fit 30 fps, and p99 sat about 0.3 ms above the median. The recording kept 80 fps throughout. With three real FLIR cameras triggered at 60 fps by an Arduino, the same 8 fit, and each camera kept all 126,000 frames of a 35-minute recording.

    A High Performance power plan, browsers and editors moved to the Intel iGPU, and nobody signed in brought the unpinned case to 13.4 / 16.2 ms, but added nothing once inference was pinned. campy-live now does this itself by default: it moves itself onto the performance cores at High priority, and campy's camera processes and encoders onto the efficiency cores once their live feed appears. In a live 3-camera recording, turning that off for 40 s from campy-live-view (its c key) raised the median from 9.3 to 11.2 ms per set.

  4. Keep the GPU's clock at full speed.

    An NVIDIA GPU picks its own clock speed from how busy it looks, and live tracking's short burst of work per frame didn't look busy. While campy-live tracked 3 mice at 30 fps, the Pro Max Slim's RTX 2000 Ada sat at 780 MHz of its 2,500, and while it waited for a recording to start it fell to its idle clock of 210 MHz. That was most of the gap to the other machines. campy-live now keeps a tiny part of the GPU busy, at the lowest priority, from the moment the cameras' feed appears: 4 of its blocks while it waits, and while it tracks, 0 to 2, as many as keep the clock up. The GPU then stays at about 2,500 MHz. On a replay of the same 3 mice, the time per set fell from 27.6 to 13.2 ms (p99 28.2 to 14.3), and 60 fps fit with nothing skipped.

    The cost is about 20 W while waiting, against 2.5 W idle, and the GPU drew about 46 W while tracking. The models' own work isn't slowed: it gets the GPU's other blocks first. It's on by default with NVIDIA and AMD GPUs (--hold-gpu-clock).

    Other GPUs slowed down too, once the work came at the cameras' pace. The earlier sweeps ran sets back to back, which kept the GPUs busier than live tracking does, so they overstated live speed. Started 60 times a second instead (the benchmark's --pace 60), with 3 cameras: the RTX 6000 Ada ran the lightest model pairs at about 825 MHz of its 2,730, and the default pair took 10.6 ms per set instead of 7.8 with its clock held. The EVO-X2's AMD GPU ran them at 2,100–2,500 MHz of its 2,900, and 11 of the 32 sizes fit 60 fps instead of 18. On the Pro Max Slim, 0 of 32 fit without the hold and 8 with it. Without the hold, heavier models kept a GPU busier and so ran at a higher clock: on the RTX 6000 Ada the light pairs were no faster than heavy ones. With the clock held, the usual order came back: the lighter, the faster. In campy-live itself on the EVO-X2 (3 cameras at 60 fps, default pair), the AMD GPU ran at 2,117 MHz without the hold and 8.9 instead of 9.9 ms per set with it, so since pull request 104 the hold is on by default with AMD's ROCm too. On the Precision's RTX 6000 Ada it was 7.8 ms with the hold against 11.1 at about 1,080 MHz without.

    The RTX 2000 Ada's slow clock went away when the Pro Max Slim restarted on 21 October, after two days up: it then kept 2,505 MHz at the cameras' pace with no help, with the driver's power mode on "Normal" or "Prefer maximum performance" alike, and the default pair took 11.2 ms. A restart didn't change the RTX 6000 Ada, though: after the Precision 7875 restarted, its light pairs still fell to about 810 MHz without the hold (default pair 10.8 ms, 7.9 with it), as before. With light models that card is idle about half of each frame, and lowering its clock then is its normal behaviour. What puts the RTX 2000 Ada into its slow state is not known yet, so leave the hold on. One spinning block costs the models about 0.3 ms per set (2 blocks, 0.55 ms) when the clock is already up, so campy-live reads the GPU's clock every half second and spins only as many blocks as the clock needs, 0 to 2 (--hold-gpu-clock-blocks auto): on the freshly restarted Pro Max Slim it spun none after its first 10 s and ran the default pair at 11.65 ms per set, against 11.53 without the hold, 11.83 with 1 block always spinning and 12.09 with 2; on the EVO-X2 and the RTX 6000 Ada it found that 1 block was needed and kept it.

  5. Keep Windows awake during recordings.

    With nobody signed in, the Pro Max Slim's screen timed out after 10 minutes and Windows entered Modern Standby in the middle of a recording. campy fell from 80 to 25 frames per second, and single inference sets stalled for up to 34 s. campy now asks Windows to stay awake while it records (on by default). When campy is started over SSH with nobody signed in, Windows refuses the "keep the screen on" part of that request, so on such a PC also set the screen to never turn off while plugged in.

    Whether the rest of campy's request alone keeps a recording at full speed through Modern Standby hasn't been tested; the screen timeout is the setting known to prevent it.

  6. On a fast GPU, run the models as CUDA graphs.

    Replaying each model's forward pass as a recorded CUDA graph (--cuda-graphs in the benchmark) made the RTX 6000 Ada's pipeline 20% faster at the median and 25% at p99: 5.2–7.8 ms across all 32 combinations, every one with p99 under 8.3 ms, half the 60 fps budget. The output was identical to running without graphs. The gain held while recording: with 3 fake cameras encoding at 60 fps the default pair went from 7.7 / 9.3 to 5.3 / 6.4 ms. At 8 cameras the RTX 6000 Ada is busy with the models themselves, so graphs gained only about 5% idle, but while recording 8 fake cameras they raised the model sizes fitting 60 fps from 14 to 21 of 32, because they remove the launch delays that the recording's CPU load adds. Started 60 times a second with the clock held, the way campy-live runs them, the default pair went from 7.9 / 9.3 to 5.3 / 6.4 ms at 3 cameras and from 9.6 / 11.5 to 8.9 / 9.7 ms at 8, and at 3 cameras 27 of the 32 sizes fit even 120 fps with graphs, against none without.

    On the RTX 2000 Ada graphs helped only 2–4%, because that card is limited by its own compute. sleap-nn doesn't use graphs on Windows by itself: torch.compile, which would, needs Triton, so the live pipeline captures them the way the benchmark does. campy-live --cuda-graphs now does: on the Pro Max Slim it took the 3-mouse replay from 13.2 to 12.4 ms per set (p99 14.3 to 13.0), and on 300 recorded sets its keypoints were identical to running without graphs. Each number of animals found gives the model a differently sized batch, and each size gets its own graph: up to 15 at 3 cameras with up to 5 animals each, and up to 40 at 8 cameras. The first set with a new count costs 22–73 ms more; campy-live's warm-up runs every count before the recording starts. Each graph kept its own GPU memory at first, and together they filled a lot of it: on the RTX 6000 Ada, campy-live's graphs held 11.2 GB at 8 cameras (1.7 GB at 3), and 28 of the benchmark's held 8.1 GB. Now all of a model's graphs share one pool of memory, in the benchmark since pull request 99 and in campy-live since pull request 107: 0.65 GB at 8 cameras and 0.26 GB at 3, with the same time per set (p50 8.7 ms at 8 cameras, 6.0 ms at 3, with up to 5 animals per camera). campy-live says how much its graphs hold when it starts.

  7. Compiling the models could make them several times faster, most of all on the AMD GPU. campy-live doesn't do it yet.

    A compiled engine fuses each convolution with the steps after it, so their results don't make a round trip through memory. Measured on the two trained models with the replay videos (networks only, 60 sets a second, clock held), a set of 3 frames with 5 mice took: on the EVO-X2, 11.1 ms in fp16 PyTorch as now and 4.3 ms with torch.compile, 2.6 times faster with the same accuracy; on the RTX 6000 Ada, 2.1 ms with CUDA graphs and 1.2 ms with TensorRT (1.7 ms with torch.compile); and on the Pro Max Slim's RTX 2000 Ada, 10.9 ms in PyTorch, 10.2 ms with CUDA graphs and 4.5 ms with TensorRT (7.4 ms with torch.compile). Details below.

    That frees about 6.5 ms of the 16.7 ms per set on both the EVO-X2 and the Pro Max Slim. On the Pro Max Slim, whose live pipeline took 16.5 ms per set with 5 mice and skipped 4–6% of the sets, that should leave plenty of room. campy-live --fixed-crops --cuda-graphs, the default now, already makes 5 mice fit there with no engine: 12.6 ms per set, nothing skipped (below). AMD's own engine (MIGraphX) gained nothing on the EVO-X2's GPU, and int8 gained 0.3 ms at most while moving keypoints. Using an engine in campy-live needs a fixed number of crops, because an engine is built for one batch size; campy-live --fixed-crops (pull request 126) always runs --max-instances crops per frame, which is what an engine needs.

  8. Choose a model with about 2 ms to spare in the benchmark.

    The live pipeline runs 1–1.7 ms slower at the median and 2–2.5 ms at p99 than the inference-only benchmark, because it also copies each set's frames in, reads the results back and logs them. On the EVO-X2 with 5 mice at 60 fps, a model needs a benchmark p99 of about 14–14.5 ms or less to keep up. The model with 160 px crops (14.0 ms in the benchmark) kept up with nothing skipped; one with 192 px crops and 24 filters (15.3 ms) still skipped a set now and then (details below).

  9. Dual-channel memory is enough, and 64 GB covers 3 cameras. Don't pay for 8 channels.

    Confined to one 6-core Threadripper chiplet, the workload had at most 57 GB/s of memory bandwidth, less than a dual-channel DDR5 desktop, and still met 60 fps. With the other chiplets pulling 172 GB/s at the same time, latency rose only 6–12%.

    Three 1280 × 1024 cameras at 80 fps are 0.3 GB/s of pixels. Inference runs in the GPU's own memory. The largest use of system memory is campy's write buffer during a disk stall: 60 s of 3 cameras at 60 fps is up to 14.2 GB, and in the live runs on the 64 GB EVO-X2 at least 56 GB stayed free or reclaimable.

  10. Record the videos to a disk of their own.

    The EVO-X2's SSD sometimes stops completing writes for 10–16 s, with nothing in the system logs: three stalls (9.6, 10 and 16 s) in about 7.5 hours of recording and disk tests. Turning off the drive's power saving didn't stop them. campy now buffers up to 60 s of frames in memory on the EVO-X2, which covers the longest stall seen so far with room to spare, and catches up about 9 times faster than the backlog builds. A separate drive for the videos removes the problem rather than absorbing it.

    For sizing that drive: H.265 on the Pro Max Slim's Quick Sync at 30 fps and quality 26 (1280 × 1024, the setting chosen after the pose models found 98.4% of the animals they found in lossless video) is 1.26 Mbit/s per camera, about 14 GB per camera per day.

  11. Prefer Linux for the inference machine if you can.

    In every EVO-X2 run (Linux, ROCm), recording included, no set took more than twice the median, and p99 stayed 0.4–1.0 ms above it. Every Windows GPU had occasional sets of twice the median or more, and the RTX 2000 and RTX 4000 Ada ran p99 3–7 ms above the median. The RTX 6000 Ada's p99 was as tight as the EVO-X2's, and so was the Pro Max Slim's once inference was pinned to its performance cores at High priority. GPU vendor and OS differ between these machines, so this is a strong hint rather than a controlled result.

  12. A Strix Halo mini-PC (EVO-X2) handles 3 cameras, but not 8.

    At 3 cameras it met 60 fps in 13–18 of 32 model combinations, recording included. With three real FLIR cameras triggered at 60 fps by an Arduino, 12–13 fit and the default pair ran at 9.2 ms median, 9.6 ms p99. With trained models it tracked every set of a 12-minute 3-camera recording from the first frame, none skipped, with 3D poses ready 13.2 ms after the last frame of a set arrived (p99 14.5 ms). With real footage of 5 mice replayed at 60 fps, the 160 px-crop model kept up with nothing skipped and 3D ready 16.8 ms after the frames (p99 19–20 ms). At 8 cameras its fastest pipeline needed 24 ms per set idle and 28 ms while recording 8 streams at 60 fps: about 35 fps. The recording itself held up: 8 × 72,000 frames over 20 minutes, none dropped, no GPU errors.

  13. Triangulation and 3D tracking cost little, and no longer add to the time per set.

    Linear (DLT) triangulation of 5 animals × 15 nodes took 0.15–0.19 ms per set with 3 cameras and 0.22–0.25 ms with 8, on every machine. Matching the animals across cameras, keeping their identities and triangulating took 0.7–1.3 ms per set for 5 mice. campy-live now does that step on its own thread while the model works on the next set, so the time per set is the longer of the two steps rather than their sum.

Hardware

  • Same on paper. GPU memory bandwidth 212 vs 206 GB/s and fp16 matrix throughput 34 vs 35 TFLOPS.
  • Convolutions differ. These models are 16-filter networks of small 3 × 3 convolutions and memory-bound steps. On a 64-channel 3 × 3 convolution, AMD's MIOpen reached 27 TFLOPS and Nvidia's cuDNN 12: cuDNN's tensor-core kernels suit wide layers. That is one shape, so read the ratio as a direction.
  • Layout conversions. cuDNN converts each activation to channels-last and back. Channels-last models cut the forward passes from 2.15 to 1.67 ms and from 7.34 to 5.52 ms, against the EVO-X2's 1.40 and 5.07.
  • A small card. The RTX 2000 Ada is a 70 W low-profile card, the smallest of Nvidia's Ada workstation line. On the RTX 6000 Ada the GPU part of the gap disappears.
  • Its clock. In the live pipeline the RTX 2000 Ada chose 780 MHz of its 2,500, because each frame's work comes as a short burst, and that doubled the time per set. campy-live now holds the clock up (recommendations above). The profiling here ran sets back to back, which keeps a GPU busier; its clocks weren't logged.
  • Hybrid CPU. The Core Ultra 9 285 has 8 performance and 16 efficiency cores; the EVO-X2 has 16 identical Zen 5 cores. While recording, the Pro Max Slim's inference slowed 21–23% (p50) against 3–7% on the EVO-X2, even though its encoders run on a separate Intel iGPU. The CPU-bound work around the models competes with campy and the encoders, and Windows moved the inference thread onto efficiency cores. Pinning it to the performance cores at High priority removed the slowdown (table below).
  • Memory is not a factor (see above).

Operating system and drivers

  • Many small steps. Around the models, sleap-nn runs about 450 tiny GPU kernels per set across the pipeline and waits for the GPU about 20 times (9 .item() and 11 nonzero calls), because each step depends on how many peaks were found.
  • Launch cost. About 11 µs of CPU time per kernel on Windows/CUDA against 5 µs on Linux/ROCm: 5.1 against 2.1 ms of CPU time per set. With frequent waits the CPU can't queue work ahead, so this lands in the latency.
  • What graphs fix. CUDA graphs remove the launches inside the models, not around them. The RTX 6000 Ada's models were launch-bound (graphs: −20–25%); the RTX 2000 Ada's were GPU-bound (graphs: −2–4%), and so was the RTX 6000 Ada at 8 cameras (−5%).
  • Tails. In every EVO-X2 run no set took twice the median and p99 stayed 0.4–1.0 ms above it. The Pro Max Slim's p99 sat 3–7 ms above its median, with occasional sets of 84–122 ms. Typical Windows sources: the WDDM GPU driver model, deferred interrupt handling, background services such as backup software, and power management, including Modern Standby (see the recommendations).
  • Not separated yet. OS and GPU vendor change together between these two machines. Booting the Pro Max Slim from an Ubuntu USB stick and rerunning on the same RTX 2000 Ada would separate them.

What could close the gap on the Pro Max Slim, cheapest first

  1. Always run the models channels-last: free, and the fastest setting on Nvidia. campy-live does this by default on Nvidia GPUs.
  2. Hold the GPU's clock up, so it doesn't settle at 780 MHz under live tracking's bursts: the biggest single gain, about half the time per set. campy-live does this by default with NVIDIA GPUs.
  3. Pin inference to the performance cores at High priority, with the recording on the efficiency cores: the biggest gain in the benchmark (table above), and now campy-live's default. A High Performance power plan and keeping desktop apps off the inference GPU help when inference isn't pinned. Nvidia's TCC driver mode, which takes a card out of Windows' display driver model, didn't work on either card it was tried on, this one included (below); stay on the usual WDDM mode.
  4. CUDA graphs (campy-live --cuda-graphs): about 6% on the RTX 2000 Ada, with identical keypoints.
  5. A faster GPU: once the GPU is fast enough, CUDA graphs bring Windows down to a 5–8 ms floor (RTX 6000 Ada). The monitor-free RTX 4000 Ada test will show whether a mid-range card gets there.
  6. Linux on the same hardware, which would also settle the OS question.
  7. Software: fewer GPU waits around the models. campy-live --fixed-crops --cuda-graphs (both on by default with a GPU since pull request 134) cuts every animal's crop in every frame, so a set runs as one graph with no wait in the middle and one copy back, with the same animals and keypoints. With 5 mice it took each set from 10.3 to 2.7 ms on the RTX 6000 Ada (frame to 3D 13.3 to 5.7 ms), and from 15.0 to 13.6 ms on the EVO-X2. On a GPU that is itself the limit, like the EVO-X2's, it pays only when about --max-instances animals are in view, since every missing one still costs a crop. On the Pro Max Slim's RTX 2000 Ada it took each set from 16.8 to 12.6 ms (p99 18.0 to 12.8), so 5 mice now fit 60 fps there: no set skipped instead of about 6%, and frame to 3D 28.6 to 15.7 ms. There it paid with any number of animals in view. With no options, campy-live now runs this way on all three machines (12.6, 2.7 and 13.6 ms per set); --fixed-crops off --cuda-graphs off gives the old way.

End to end with trained models, on the EVO-X2

The benchmark above times inference alone with untrained models. These tests ran the whole live pipeline instead: campy recorded three real FLIR cameras triggered at 60 fps, and campy-live took every synchronized set, ran 2D pose inference, matched animals across cameras and triangulated them in 3D, for 12 minutes (43,200 sets). The first two runs used a centroid model at 0.25 input scale (16 filters, max stride 16, output stride 2), untrained and then trained, with a centered-instance model on 192 px crops (24 filters, max stride 16). The third ran the current code, which loads the models before the cameras start and does the 3D step alongside inference, with the same centroid model and a centered-instance model on 160 px crops (24 filters, max stride 32). "Frame to 3D pose" is measured from the moment the last of a set's three frames arrived.

The first two runs' 1-in-1,000 frame-to-3D times were 13.7 and 14.6 ms; the third run reported p99 instead.

  • Every set was ready within one frame interval. The trained models left about 4 ms of the 16.7 ms budget in all three runs.
  • The slow first set is fixed. It used to take about 200 ms, because the warm-up ran on blank frames, which never exercise the steps that crop around each animal found. The warm-up now runs the whole model with every possible number of animals found, so on three real cameras the first set took 13.4–14.7 ms and none were skipped. No need to start recording early for it.
  • Start campy-live before the recording. It now loads and warms up its models first, which takes 5–18 s depending on the machine, then waits for the cameras, so it analyses the recording from frame 1. Before this change it missed about the first 5 s of every recording.
  • The 3D positions here only show timing. These cameras weren't calibrated and no animals were in view: two overexposed cameras gave the models 4–5 false detections per frame, and the third gave none, so the models handled about 9 crops per set instead of the 15 that 5 mice give. The 5-mouse replay below is the test at full load.
  • Disk. No SSD pauses happened during these runs. Pauses of up to 16 s happened at other times on the same machine; campy's 60 s write buffer and background saving of logs and frame times keep such pauses from stalling recording or inference.

  • Being just over the frame time costs a whole frame of delay. The EVO-X2's inference is only 1.5 ms faster, but it finishes each set before the next frame comes, so its 3D poses are ready 16.8 ms after the frame. On the Pro Max Slim each set ends just after the next frame has arrived, so that frame waits (10 ms at the median, up to 18 ms) and the set after it is skipped: frame to 3D pose took 28 ms. A model that leaves room, as the 2 ms rule of thumb above says, avoids this.
  • The Precision 7875 has room to spare. Its RTX 6000 Ada took 10.0 ms of the 16.7 ms between frames (p99 12.4–12.7 ms), so no set was skipped or misaligned and each set was done before the next frame came; 3D took 1.1 ms more. Measured exactly, each frame's 3D pose was ready 12.9 ms after the frame reached the computer (p99 15.5 ms): within one frame, and 4 ms sooner than on the EVO-X2.
  • Recording three cameras on the Precision 7875 changes little. With three emulated cameras encoded by NVENC on the RTX 4000 Ada, inference took 0.8 ms more at the median (10.8 ms, p99 14.5), no set was skipped or misaligned, frame to 3D pose was 13.3 ms (p99 17.4), and each camera kept all 3,600 frames. This is the test the guide had listed as not yet done; it used emulated cameras, not real ones.
  • The 60 s recordings agree with the longer runs. The Pro Max Slim skipped 5.9% of sets with campy's own emulated cameras, between its replays (4.0–4.5%) and its 10-minute recording with the test copy (6.5%), with the same inference time. The EVO-X2, encoding on the GPU that also runs the models, took 0.4 ms more inference than in its replay and skipped 3 sets of 3,600.

  • 3D costs about 1 ms per set, run alongside the next set's inference. Recording costs inference about 0.4 ms and a few more skipped sets.
  • The recording was never affected. Each camera kept all 36,000 frames, with no gaps in the frame numbers, pinned or not.
  • For 5 mice at 60 fps on this PC, run campy-live --max-instances 5: fixed crops and CUDA graphs are on by default. With them off, use a lighter centered-instance model, such as the 16-filter 192 px one (12.0 / 13.1 ms on the EVO-X2, above), or record at 30 fps. CUDA graphs saved 6% with 3 mice, which would bring 16.5 ms only to about 15.5, still without the 2 ms of room.

  • On the EVO-X2, torch.compile is 2.6 times faster than today's PyTorch. The compiled networks launch about 80 GPU programs per crop batch instead of 131 and keep the GPU busy 2.9 ms instead of 8.8. The biggest single saving is the upsampling, which PyTorch runs slowly on this GPU (3.4 of 11.8 ms in fp32). Its three modes gave the same time there. Storing the models' tensors channels-last, the change that helps NVIDIA cards, gave nothing (11.7 ms).
  • AMD's MIGraphX didn't help on the EVO-X2. Its fp16 programs took as long as PyTorch (11.1 ms); this GPU isn't one MIGraphX is tuned for. Its int8 mode produced exactly the same output as its fp16 mode, so it never ran in int8 here.
  • On NVIDIA, TensorRT is fastest, then torch.compile (max-autotune mode), then CUDA graphs. torch.compile on Windows needs triton-windows. TensorRT needs one engine per crop count, built in 9–25 s each.
  • int8 isn't worth it. TensorRT int8, calibrated on 100 sets of real frames, was no faster than its fp16 on the RTX 6000 Ada and only slightly faster on the RTX 4000 Ada (the networks are too small for int8 to pay much), and it moved 15.6% of keypoints by more than 1 pixel, with p99 4.4 px. On the RTX 2000 Ada TensorRT couldn't build the int8 centered-instance engine (a CUDA driver memory error, every time).
  • Accuracy, fp16 engines. Against full-precision PyTorch on 500 sets (89,000 keypoints), every fp16 engine moved the keypoints by 0.01 px or less at p99. A few (0.5–1%) jumped further, between two nearly equal spots on the confidence map, as fp16 PyTorch does today. TensorRT fp16 moved them a little more (about 1% over 1 px; p99 0.02–2.8 px depending on the card), because it runs the whole network in fp16.
  • fp16 drops 0.6–0.9% of centroids, in PyTorch too. sleap-nn keeps a centroid only where a pixel is strictly higher than all 8 of its neighbours. In fp16 the two top pixels sometimes round to the same value, and that mouse then has no centroid in that frame: 45–64 of 7,466, always with exactly two tied pixels. Full precision never did this. A peak finder that accepts ties would fix it.
  • Weights don't change the timing. The same networks with random weights took the same time with CUDA graphs and the compiled engines (within 0.05 ms). Plain PyTorch, which the CPU limits on these GPUs, varied by up to 0.7 ms from run to run either way.

How this was measured, and what it doesn't cover

  • Benchmark: bench/sleap_infer_bench.py in the campy repo, timing sleap-nn 0.3.3's TopDownLayer.predict on 3 or 8 uint8 1280 × 1024 frames per set, with a device sync after every set.
  • Except in the end-to-end tests and the 5-mouse replay, models are untrained (random weights, built from sleap-nn configs). Run time doesn't depend on the weights; the centroid output is replaced with 5 peaks per frame so cropping always sees 5 animals.
  • Most tests used one real FLIR camera; the other "cameras" were ffmpeg encoding a looped FLIR clip in real time with campy's encoder settings, which load the encoder and CPU but not USB. Those recordings ran at 80 fps (3 cameras) and 60 fps (8 cameras on the EVO-X2). Each condition was measured once.
  • Both 3-camera rigs were then retested with three real FLIR Blackfly S cameras (BFS-U3-13Y3M), triggered at 60 fps by an Arduino running campy's trigger sketch, recording 35 minutes while the full sweep ran: the inference results matched the fake-camera runs. On the EVO-X2 this was done twice, plus once recording alone.
  • The 5-mouse replay decoded 60 s (3,600 frames per camera) of a recorded session into campy's live feed at 60 fps. The old-vs-current comparison uses a replay that creates the feed 3 s before the first frame, as campy does before the trigger. Runs that overlapped other GPU work were discarded and repeated; later runs on the EVO-X2 hold a lock that checks nothing else touches the GPU or the trigger.
  • The Pro Max Slim's live run used a separate software stack from its benchmark (torch 2.11.0+cu128, sleap-nn 0.3.3).
  • The Precision 7875's live runs used campy 2.1.0rc5 with torch 2.14.1+cu130 and sleap-nn 0.3.3 (the same torch as its benchmark), with campy-live on the RTX 6000 Ada and the monitor on the RTX 4000 Ada. A shorter check recorded one emulated camera with NVENC on the RTX 4000 Ada while campy-live tracked it on the RTX 6000 Ada: 0 of 1,188 sets skipped, inference 9.6 / 12.3 ms, all 1,200 frames recorded. In 2.1.0rc5 three emulated cameras each start on their own, so that check used one. The 22 October runs used campy 2.1.0rc6, whose emulated cameras start together (pull request 119), with the same software otherwise; the one-camera check then gave 0 of 1,197 sets skipped, inference 9.0 / 12.5 ms, frame to keypoints 9.8 / 13.7 ms.
  • The Pro Max Slim's 5-mouse replays fed the three views straight into the live feed, recording nothing. Its recording runs used campy's emulated cameras (cameraMake: "emu"), which load the CPU and the encoder like real cameras but not USB, from a copy of campy with two test-only changes: each emulated camera gave one gray channel, as the FLIR cameras do, and all three started their first frame at the same moment, as a trigger makes them. Each time the 60 s clip started again, the emulated cameras paused 110–150 ms (9 times in each 10-minute run); no frame was lost. campy 2.1.0rc6 does all three itself (pull request 119): one gray channel, one shared start, and a loop with no pause. The 22 October recordings used it unchanged.
  • Windows' clock. campy's hosttimes.npy and campy-live's timestamps came from Python's time.monotonic, which on Windows, up to Python 3.12, moves in steps of 15.6 ms. So on the Pro Max Slim those times, and the latencies campy-live reported from them, were rounded to 15–16 ms steps (hence the "15.6 ms clock steps" notes above). Inference times were never affected. Since pull request 118, campy takes those times from Windows' precise clock (QueryPerformanceCounter, which Python 3.13 uses for time.monotonic), the same in every program, so latencies measured after it are exact. The 5-mouse runs above already used that clock, through a test-only switch, so theirs are exact too, as are all of 22 October's. A test program that writes frames into the live feed itself, like the replays here, must stamp them with the same clock (campy.clock.HostNs): stamped with time.monotonic_ns, a different clock, the Windows replays' latencies came out 40–56 ms, which meant nothing. The 22 October replays used HostNs.
  • Windows machines run the GPUs under WDDM; no kernel-log equivalent of Linux's amdgpu messages was checked there.
  • Pinning and the Windows changes were measured on the four fastest model pairs in two 21-minute recordings, before and after the changes, with a backup service running in both; the second recording's last minutes fell into Modern Standby and were discarded. All 32 model sizes were then measured pinned, in a separate recording with the backup service stopped and the screen timeout set to Never.
  • The 21 October runs on the Pro Max Slim logged the GPU's clock every 50 ms, and held a lock so that no other program used the GPU meanwhile; runs that overlapped another session's were discarded and repeated. The live runs' 1 animal per set makes them lighter than the 3-mouse replays.
  • That lock was agreed between sessions by hand. Since pull request 97, bench/rig.py does it on both Linux and Windows: it runs one test at a time, logs anything else that used the GPU, the cameras, the encoder, the trigger, the CPU or the disk meanwhile (each run's log ends RIG_CLEAN or RIG_CONTAMINATED), and logs the GPU's clock and what held it down. New measurements should be taken through it, and compared only between clean runs at similar clocks.
  • CUDA graphs were measured in the benchmark idle and while recording, at 3 and 8 cameras, on the Precision 7875 (fake cameras encoding with NVENC on the RTX 4000 Ada, none of their frames dropped) and on the Pro Max Slim (3 real FLIR cameras at 60 fps, all 252,000 frames per camera kept), and in the live pipeline on the Pro Max Slim (above). Graphs' output was identical to running without them in every run, including 300–400 sets with a random 0–5 animals per frame.
  • The Pro Max Slim's benchmark runs of 20–21 October ran with the RTX 2000 Ada at about 900–1,000 MHz on the light model sizes, because the card had fallen into a slow state and the benchmark didn't yet hold the clock as campy-live does (it can now: --hold-clock on). A restart that afternoon ended the slow state (recommendations above). They were about 1.8 times slower than the same runs on 19 October (default pair 19.7 against 11.5 ms), so they compare graphs on and off at that low clock (1–8% faster with graphs) and don't show the card's capability; this guide's other Pro Max Slim benchmark numbers come from 19 October. The Precision 7875's RTX 6000 Ada didn't slow down with sets run back to back: its default pair took 7.3 ms idle on both days. At the cameras' pace it did (recommendations above).
  • TCC driver mode failed on both NVIDIA workstation cards it was tried on, each with no monitor attached: the Precision 7875's RTX 6000 Ada and the Pro Max Slim's RTX 2000 Ada (driver 596.71, Windows 11 25H2). The same thing happened on both. After a card was switched to TCC (nvidia-smi -dm 1 for that card, then a reboot), Windows couldn't start it: Device Manager reported "insufficient resources" (Code 10), and nvidia-smi could no longer reach it, so it couldn't switch it back either. Each card was restored to WDDM by removing the driver-mode value from its registry entry and rebooting, and both work normally again. The RTX 4000 Ada can't use TCC, because it drives the monitor and the Threadripper has no graphics of its own. Don't count on TCC for Windows workstation cards: CUDA graphs already remove most of the per-launch cost it aims at. If you try it anyway, switch the card by its bus ID, note how to undo it first, and expect a reboot each way.
  • Not yet tested: the RTX 4000 Ada with no monitor attached, and the Precision 7875 recording real cameras while it tracks (three emulated ones were tested on 22 October, above).
