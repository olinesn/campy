# Hardware for live tracking

What to buy for a whole rig (cameras, lenses, cables, trigger, lights, computer, disk and calibration board, with prices) is the [shopping list](#what-to-buy) at the end of this page. The rest is about the computer.

The full guide, with every measurement, is [hardware-guide.html](hardware-guide.html). GitHub shows that file as source code; to read it formatted, open the [published copy](https://claude.ai/artifact/1iFm1x157Wfw4rrHQf2FTk) or download the file and open it in a browser. It compares three machines running campy and sleap-nn together:

- **GMKtec EVO-X2**: mini-PC, AMD Ryzen AI Max+ 395 with its Radeon 8060S integrated GPU, Ubuntu 24.04 and ROCm.
- **Dell Pro Max Slim FCS1250**: Intel Core Ultra 9 285 (8 performance + 16 efficiency cores), NVIDIA RTX 2000 Ada, Windows 11 and CUDA.
- **Dell Precision 7875**: AMD Threadripper PRO 7965WX, NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada (plus an RTX 4000 Ada for the monitor), Windows 11 and CUDA. Since 21 October 2026 it is also set up for live tracking (campy's dev branch with pull request 147, compiled networks by default, since 22 October), and has run the 5-mouse replay with 3D, and recorded three emulated cameras while tracking them.

## The short version

This list leaves the numbers to the guide, so it can't fall out of date with it.

1. **With compiled models every GPU tested keeps up with 3 cameras, with room to spare; 8 cameras is where they differ.** `campy-live` compiles the models by default (TensorRT on NVIDIA, `torch.compile` on AMD). Then every one of the guide's 32 model sizes fits 60 fps at 3 cameras on all four GPUs, and the small 70 W NVIDIA card and the EVO-X2's integrated AMD GPU are about as fast as each other. At 8 cameras and 60 fps the large NVIDIA cards fit (nearly) every size; the small card and the EVO-X2 fit the lighter quarter, and run the trained 5-mouse models with almost nothing to spare. At 30 fps all of them fit 8 cameras.
2. **A GPU slows its own clock** when the work comes in short bursts, as live tracking's does. The NVIDIA cards tested did, the AMD one less, though the Pro Max Slim's card stopped after a restart (the Precision's RTX 6000 Ada didn't). `campy-live` now keeps the GPU at full speed by default, NVIDIA or AMD (`--hold-gpu-clock`), and spins nothing when the clock stays up by itself. With compiled models it matters more: a set is so short that the GPU looks idle, and without the hold the RTX 6000 Ada took over 7 times as long per set. Benchmark at the cameras' pace with the clock held (`--pace 60 --hold-clock on`), as `campy-live` runs; sets run back to back flatter the GPU.
3. **Fast CPU cores matter more than many cores**, and with compiled models the CPU matters much less than before: `campy-live` launches each set as one CUDA graph, and squeezing the recording and inference onto 6 cores no longer slowed it. Leave a core for `campy-live-view` if you'll watch the tracking.
4. **On an Intel hybrid CPU, run inference on the performance cores** at High priority, with the recording on the efficiency cores. `campy-live` does both by default; with compiled models it gains only a little at p99, but costs nothing. On Windows, on any CPU, it also keeps itself off the CPU that handles its GPU's interrupts, where every kernel launch waits ([Live tracking](live-tracking.md#on-windows-nvidia-cuda)).
5. **Keep Windows awake.** campy asks Windows to stay awake while it records (`keepAwake`), but a PC with nobody signed in still needs its screen timeout set to Never.
6. **Leave about 2.5 ms of headroom.** The benchmark now times a set as `campy-live` runs it, and gives the same time per set; getting the frames to it and the results out adds 1–2.5 ms, and the 3D another 1–1.5. At 60 fps pick a model whose benchmark p99 stays under about 14 ms ([Models](models.md)).
7. **Dual-channel memory is enough.** Memory bandwidth didn't limit anything. 64 GB covers 3 cameras, including campy's buffer for disk stalls.
8. **Record videos to a disk of their own.** Some SSDs stop completing writes for many seconds. campy's write buffer (`writeQueueMaxSec`) absorbs that, but a separate drive avoids it.
9. **Linux and Windows are both steady with compiled models**: p99 within about 1 ms of the median on every GPU. With sleap-nn's own layer and uncompiled models, Linux had been clearly steadier.
10. **The EVO-X2 handles 3 cameras at 60 fps with 5 mice easily, and 8 cameras with a light model** (the trained 5-mouse models at 8 cameras are right at the limit of 60 fps). The Pro Max Slim keeps up with 3 cameras at 60 fps with 3 mice, but, with both off, not with 5 on the same models: its GPU needs about as long as one frame per set, so it skips a few percent of the sets and its 3D poses come about 28 ms after the frames, against the EVO-X2's 17. With fixed crops and CUDA graphs, both on by default with a GPU now, it keeps up with 5: 12.6 ms per set, nothing skipped, 3D poses 15.8 ms after the frames (see live-tracking.md, "Fixed crops"). With `--fixed-crops off`, give it a lighter centered-instance model for 5 mice at 60 fps. With a fourth camera (26 October), recording four at 30 fps with 3 mice, it tracked every set but the first in 3D, 8.3 ms after the frames at the median and 10.4 ms at p99 ([Live tracking](live-tracking.md#four-cameras-on-the-pro-max-slim-26-october-2026)).
11. **3D tracking is cheap**, and it runs alongside inference, so it doesn't add to the time per set. On a fast GPU with compiled models it now takes about as long as inference.
12. **Compiled models are about twice as fast** (more at 8 cameras), and `campy-live` uses them by default (`--engine auto`): `torch.compile` on the EVO-X2's AMD GPU, TensorRT on NVIDIA cards, where they most help the smaller ones. int8 isn't worth it ([Models](models.md)).

## Measuring a new machine

`bench/sleap_infer_bench.py` times the models, untrained, so you can test a machine before any labelling. Its docstring explains the options; `--sweep` runs the 32 model sizes the guide reports. Add `--fixed-crops --cuda-graphs --engine auto --cpus auto --precision fp16 --pace 60 --hold-clock on` to time each set as `campy-live` runs it, compiled models included (it needs campy installed, or its folder on `PYTHONPATH`); `--trained CENTROID INSTANCE` times your own trained models the same way. Then run the whole pipeline on real cameras ([Live tracking](live-tracking.md)), because that is what has to keep up. To test the same thing with real animals but no cameras, campy's emulated cameras (`cameraMake: "emu"`) play recorded videos as if they were cameras: `campy-acquire` records them with your encoder settings and copies every frame into the live feed, with frame n reaching the computer at the same moment on every camera, as with a hardware trigger ([Live tracking](live-tracking.md#testing-without-cameras), and the [Recording with campy-acquire](acquire.md#emulated-cameras-cameramake-emu)). The scripts that write recorded video straight into the live feed, without recording it, aren't in campy yet.

When the machine is shared, run every timing test through `bench/rig.py` (on Linux or Windows): it runs one test at a time, says whether anything else used the GPU, the cameras, the encoder, the trigger, the CPU or the disk during it (`RIG_CLEAN` or `RIG_CONTAMINATED`), and logs the GPU's clock. Compare only clean runs, at the same GPU clock: on the Dell Pro Max Slim FCS1250, overlapping runs took a model pair from 27.6 to 42.7 ms per set, and the GPU's clock alone made a factor of two (above). The [Development](development.md#timing-tests-on-a-shared-machine-benchrigpy) explains it.

### Choosing lenses

A lens's focal length sets how much of the arena a camera sees. On the BFS-U3-13Y3M (a 1/2" sensor, 6.14 mm wide), the width a camera sees is its distance × 6.14 / the focal length:

| 4 mm | 75° | 77 cm | 154 cm | Edmund Optics UC Series #33-300, about $299 ([Edmund Optics](https://www.edmundoptics.com/p/4mm-uc-series-fixed-focal-length-lens/2966/)) |

| 8 mm | 42° | 38 cm | 77 cm | Computar M0814-MP2, about $139 ([B&H](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/888985-REG/computar_M0814_MP2_2_3_Fixed_Lens.html)); M30.5 filter thread |
| 12 mm | 29° | 26 cm | 51 cm | Computar M1214-MP2, about $133 ([B&H](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/888992-REG/computar_M1214_MP2_2_3_Fixed_Lens.html)); M30.5 filter thread |

Lenses made for 2/3" sensors, such as the Computar MP2 series, fit the 1/2" sensor too. To screw an IR-pass filter on, the lens needs a filter thread. Most cameras on the 8-camera rigs LUC3D was tested on see 75–80° across (a 4 mm lens on this camera), with an 8 mm lens on a camera that stands farther back. Fit the arena to the frame with a little margin; pixels outside it are wasted. Take lenses rated for the sensor (1/2" or larger), with locking screws on focus and iris: a calibration holds only while they stay put ([Calibration](calibration.md#before-calibrating-lens-and-exposure)). Where to put the cameras, and how many to buy, is in [Calibration](calibration.md#placing-3-cameras) and in LUC3D's [Choosing Cameras & Boards](https://talmolab.github.io/luc3d-docs/user-guide/cameras-and-boards/#how-many-cameras).

### USB

A 1280 × 1024 mono camera sends 79 MB/s at 60 fps (223 MB/s at its full 170 fps). campy's launch check budgets 300 MB/s for each 5 Gb/s USB controller ([Scaling the number of cameras](encoding.md#scaling-the-number-of-cameras)), so one controller takes 3 such cameras at 60 fps, or 7 at 30 fps. The Pro Max Slim records its four at 30 fps (157 MB/s) on its one built-in controller. For more cameras, add a PCIe card with one controller per port. On Linux, give USB more buffer memory for FLIR cameras: `usbcore.usbfs_memory_mb=1000` on the kernel's command line (the default 16 MB drops frames; [Jetson AGX Orin](jetson.md) shows where to set it).

USB 3 reaches about 5 m on a passive cable. Beyond that, use an active cable, and test each one with its camera at the full frame rate before relying on it.

### Lighting

Mice are often recorded under infrared (850 nm), which they barely see, so the light can stay on through their dark phase. A mono camera without an IR-cut filter sees 850 nm, though less well than visible light; an IR-pass filter on each lens then blocks the room lights, so the picture stays the same when they change. Whatever the light, it must not flicker: with the short exposures that keep moving animals sharp (the Pro Max Slim's rig uses 200 µs), a PWM-dimmed LED makes the brightness jump from frame to frame. Use lights driven by constant current, or dimmed by it. Aim for the brightness your pose model was trained on (an image mean of about 15–35 out of 255 for the lab's models, [Calibration](calibration.md#before-calibrating-lens-and-exposure)), and give every camera the same exposure, evening them out with gain.
